Hour 1–4 · Correlation coefficient · Univariate/bivariate/multivariate plots · Probability

The Correlation Coefficient Hour 2

Start from the question, not the formula. Last week you learned to describe one column of numbers on its own (its mean, its spread). But real datasets have many columns, and a huge question in data science is: "when this one goes up, what happens to that one?" Do people who study more hours tend to score higher? Do houses with more bedrooms tend to cost more? Correlation is a single number that answers "how strongly, and in which direction, do two variables move together?"

Reading the number. The correlation coefficient (usually written r, specifically "Pearson's r") always falls between −1 and +1:

  • +1 — perfect positive relationship: as one variable rises, the other rises in perfect lockstep.
  • 0 — no linear relationship at all: knowing one variable tells you nothing about the other.
  • −1 — perfect negative relationship: as one variable rises, the other falls in perfect lockstep.

Real-world data is almost never a perfect +1 or −1 — you'll typically see values like 0.7 (fairly strong positive), −0.3 (weak negative), or 0.05 (essentially no relationship).

import numpy as np

study_hours = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]
exam_scores = [50, 55, 65, 70, 78, 85]

correlation_matrix = np.corrcoef(study_hours, exam_scores)
print(correlation_matrix[0, 1])   # ≈ 0.99 — very strong positive relationship
The single most important warning in all of statistics Correlation does not mean causation. Ice cream sales and drowning incidents are strongly correlated — but ice cream doesn't cause drowning. Both simply rise in summer. A third, hidden factor (heat) drives both. Always ask "could something else explain this?" before assuming one variable causes the other.

Univariate, Bivariate & Multivariate Plots Hour 3

These three words simply describe how many variables a chart is showing at once — "uni" = one, "bi" = two, "multi" = many.

Univariate (one variable) — used to understand the shape and spread of a single column on its own, exactly like last week's mean/median/mode/std-dev, but visual: a histogram (bars showing how many values fall in each range) or a box plot (showing median, quartiles, and outliers at a glance).

Bivariate (two variables) — used to explore the relationship between two columns, exactly what correlation measures numerically above; a scatter plot is the classic tool, with one variable on each axis, and correlation is essentially "how close to a straight line does this scatter plot look."

Multivariate (many variables at once) — used when two variables alone don't tell the full story; a heatmap of correlations between every pair of columns, or a scatter plot where color/size encode a third variable, lets you spot patterns across an entire dataset simultaneously.

# univariate — histogram of one column
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
plt.hist(exam_scores)
plt.show()

# bivariate — scatter plot of two columns
plt.scatter(study_hours, exam_scores)
plt.xlabel("Study hours"); plt.ylabel("Exam score")
plt.show()

# multivariate — correlation heatmap across many columns (using seaborn, Week 4)
# import seaborn as sns
# sns.heatmap(dataframe.corr(), annot=True)

Introduction to Probability Hour 4

The everyday version, first. You already reason with probability constantly: "there's a good chance it rains today," "it's unlikely I'll win the lottery." Probability just formalizes "how likely" into a precise number between 0 and 1 (or equivalently, 0% to 100%): 0 means "impossible," 1 means "certain," and 0.5 means "equally likely to happen or not."

favorable_outcomes = 1     # rolling a 6
total_outcomes = 6          # faces on a die
probability = favorable_outcomes / total_outcomes
print(probability)   # ≈ 0.1667, i.e. about 16.7% chance
Why probability matters for AI/ML A spam filter doesn't say "this email IS spam" with total certainty — it computes the probability this email is spam (e.g. 0.92) and only then applies a rule like "if probability > 0.5, label it spam." Almost every ML classification model is, under the hood, a probability-estimating machine. Understanding probability now makes Machine Learning in Week 5 far less mysterious.

Probability — Joint, Conditional & Distributions

Hour 1–4 · Joint/marginal/conditional probability · Probability distributions · Discrete & continuous · Bayesian probability

Joint, Marginal & Conditional Probability Hour 1

Set the scene. Imagine a simple survey of 100 people recording two things: whether they own a car, and whether they own a house. We can ask three different, related questions about this data — and each has its own name.

Joint probability — "what's the chance of BOTH things being true at once?" e.g. P(owns a car AND owns a house). Written P(A ∩ B).

Marginal probability — "what's the chance of just ONE thing, ignoring the other entirely?" e.g. P(owns a car), regardless of house ownership. It's called "marginal" because historically these totals were written in the margins of a table.

Conditional probability — "GIVEN that one thing is already true, what's the chance of the other?" e.g. P(owns a house | owns a car) — read the vertical bar as "given." This is often the most useful of the three, because it captures how knowing one fact updates your belief about another.

# 100 people surveyed:
#              Owns House   No House
# Owns Car          30          20
# No Car            10          40

joint = 30 / 100                          # P(car AND house) = 0.30
marginal_car = (30 + 20) / 100             # P(car) = 0.50
conditional = 30 / (30 + 20)               # P(house | car) = 0.60

print(joint, marginal_car, conditional)
Real world application Netflix asking "given that you watched this sci-fi movie, what's the probability you'll like that one?" is exactly a conditional-probability question — the entire recommendation-engine industry is built on conditional probability at scale.

Probability Distributions Hour 2

The idea in one sentence. A probability distribution is a complete map of "every possible outcome, and how likely each one is" — instead of one single probability number, it's the whole picture at once. Think of it as a more detailed cousin of last week's histogram, but describing an idealized, theoretical pattern rather than one specific dataset.

Distributions split into the same two families you learned in Week 2's data types:

  • Discrete distributions — for countable outcomes (number of heads in 10 coin flips, number of defective items in a batch).
  • Continuous distributions — for outcomes that can be any value in a range (height, temperature, time until a machine fails).

Discrete Probability Distributions Hour 3–4

The most common discrete distributions you'll encounter, building from simplest to more general:

  • Bernoulli distribution — the simplest possible case: a single yes/no trial with only two outcomes (success/failure), like one coin flip.
  • Binomial distribution — repeats a Bernoulli trial n times and asks "what's the probability of exactly k successes?" e.g. flipping a coin 10 times, what's the probability of getting exactly 6 heads?
  • Poisson distribution — models how many times a rare event happens in a fixed window of time or space, e.g. how many customer support tickets arrive per hour.
from scipy.stats import binom

# probability of exactly 6 heads in 10 fair coin flips
p = binom.pmf(k=6, n=10, p=0.5)
print(p)   # ≈ 0.205

Continuous Probability Distributions Hour 3–4

The single most important continuous distribution in all of statistics and machine learning is the Normal (Gaussian) distribution — the familiar symmetric "bell curve." An enormous number of real-world measurements (height, exam scores, measurement errors) naturally follow this shape: most values cluster near the mean, and values become progressively rarer the further you move from it in either direction.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

data = np.random.normal(loc=70, scale=10, size=1000)   # mean=70, std dev=10
plt.hist(data, bins=30)
plt.show()
Connecting back to Week 2 Remember Z-scores from last week? They only make full intuitive sense in the context of a normal distribution — the "68-95-99.7 rule" says roughly 68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, 95% within 2, and 99.7% within 3. This is why a Z-score of 3 is considered an extreme outlier.

Bayesian Probability Hour 3–4

The everyday intuition first. You update your beliefs constantly as new evidence arrives. Before a medical test, you might think "there's a 1% chance I have this condition." After testing positive, that belief should change — but by how much depends on how accurate the test is. Bayes' theorem is the precise, mathematical rule for exactly how much a new piece of evidence should update an existing belief.

# Bayes' theorem: P(A|B) = P(B|A) * P(A) / P(B)
# "updated belief in A, given evidence B"

# classic example: disease testing
p_disease = 0.01                 # 1% of population has the disease (prior belief)
p_positive_given_disease = 0.99   # test is 99% accurate for people who ARE sick
p_positive_given_healthy = 0.05   # but has a 5% false-positive rate

p_positive = (p_positive_given_disease * p_disease) + \
             (p_positive_given_healthy * (1 - p_disease))

p_disease_given_positive = (p_positive_given_disease * p_disease) / p_positive
print(p_disease_given_positive)   # ≈ 0.166 — only ~16.6%, surprisingly low!
Why this result is so counter-intuitive (and so important) Even with a "99% accurate" test, testing positive only means a ~16.6% real chance of having a rare disease — because the disease is so rare (1%) that false positives from the much larger healthy population outnumber true positives. This exact reasoning pattern — "prior belief + new evidence → updated belief" — is the conceptual backbone of Naive Bayes classifiers, spam filters, and much of modern machine learning.

NumPy — Creating & Working with Arrays

Hour 1–4 · Intro to NumPy · Creating arrays · Array attributes & methods · Operations on arrays

Introduction to NumPy — Why Not Just Use Python Lists? Hour 1

The problem NumPy solves. Python's built-in lists (from Week 1) are flexible but slow for heavy number-crunching, because each element can be a totally different type and Python has to check that every time. Real data science involves millions of numbers — image pixels, sensor readings, model weights — and needs to process them fast. NumPy ("Numerical Python") introduces the array: a grid of numbers, all the same type, stored efficiently in memory and processed using highly optimized code written in C under the hood — often 10 to 100 times faster than an equivalent Python list loop.

import numpy as np    # the universal convention: import numpy as np
Why this matters for the rest of the course NumPy arrays are the foundation everything else sits on: Pandas DataFrames (Day 5 of this week) are built from NumPy arrays under the hood, and every image, audio clip, or numeric dataset fed into a machine learning or deep learning model is first converted into a NumPy (or NumPy-like) array.

Creating NumPy Arrays Hour 2–4

From a Python list — the most direct way to turn data you already have into an array:

arr = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
print(arr)          # [1 2 3 4 5]
print(type(arr))     # <class 'numpy.ndarray'>

# a 2D array (a "matrix") — a list of lists
matrix = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])
print(matrix)

From built-in generator methods — for when you need an array of a specific shape or pattern, without typing every value by hand:

zeros = np.zeros((3, 3))         # a 3x3 grid of all zeros
ones = np.ones((2, 4))            # a 2x4 grid of all ones
identity = np.eye(3)               # a 3x3 identity matrix (1s on the diagonal)
sequence = np.arange(0, 10, 2)     # [0 2 4 6 8] — like Python's range(), but an array
evenly_spaced = np.linspace(0, 1, 5)  # 5 evenly spaced numbers from 0 to 1

From random generation — essential for simulations, and for initializing a neural network's starting weights later in the course:

random_uniform = np.random.rand(3, 3)        # random values between 0 and 1
random_ints = np.random.randint(1, 100, 5)     # 5 random integers from 1 to 99
random_normal = np.random.randn(3, 3)          # random values from a normal distribution

Array Attributes & Methods Hour 2–4

Once you have an array, NumPy gives you quick built-in ways to inspect and summarize it — think of these as the array "introducing itself":

arr = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])

print(arr.shape)     # (2, 3) — 2 rows, 3 columns
print(arr.ndim)       # 2 — number of dimensions
print(arr.size)       # 6 — total number of elements
print(arr.dtype)      # int64 — the data type of every element

print(arr.max())      # 6 — largest value
print(arr.min())      # 1 — smallest value
print(arr.argmax())   # 5 — the INDEX (position) of the largest value, flattened
print(arr.argmin())   # 0 — the index of the smallest value

reshaped = arr.reshape(3, 2)   # reorganize the SAME 6 numbers into a new 3x2 shape
print(reshaped)
Common mistake .max() tells you the largest value; .argmax() tells you where it is (its index). Beginners frequently reach for the wrong one — remember: "arg" as in "argument," meaning "the input/position that produces this result."

Operations on Arrays — Copying, Editing & Combining Hour 2–4

# copying — IMPORTANT: arr2 = arr1 does NOT copy, it just points to the same data!
arr1 = np.array([1, 2, 3])
arr2 = arr1.copy()          # a true, independent copy
arr2[0] = 99
print(arr1)                  # [1 2 3] — untouched, because we used .copy()

# append and insert
arr = np.array([1, 2, 3])
appended = np.append(arr, 4)              # [1 2 3 4]
inserted = np.insert(arr, 1, 99)           # [1 99 2 3] — insert 99 at index 1

# sorting
unsorted = np.array([3, 1, 4, 1, 5])
print(np.sort(unsorted))    # [1 1 3 4 5]

# removing / deleting
removed = np.delete(arr, 0)   # removes the element at index 0 → [2 3]

# combining / concatenating
a = np.array([1, 2])
b = np.array([3, 4])
combined = np.concatenate([a, b])   # [1 2 3 4]

# splitting
parts = np.split(np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]), 3)   # 3 equal chunks: [1,2] [3,4] [5,6]
Why the copy warning matters so much This "shared reference" trap is one of the most common real-world NumPy bugs — code that silently modifies data it wasn't supposed to touch, because two variable names were secretly pointing at the exact same underlying array in memory. Always use .copy() when you need a genuinely independent version.

NumPy — Indexing, Broadcasting & Math Operations

Hour 1–4 · Data loading/saving · Indexing & selection · Broadcasting · Type casting · Arithmetic · Universal functions

Data Loading & Saving Hour 1–2

Real data doesn't start out as a hand-typed list — it lives in files. NumPy can load and save arrays directly to disk, in both plain-text and NumPy's own efficient binary format:

# saving an array to disk
arr = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
np.save("my_array.npy", arr)           # NumPy's own fast binary format
np.savetxt("my_array.csv", arr, delimiter=",")   # plain text / CSV

# loading it back
loaded = np.load("my_array.npy")
loaded_csv = np.loadtxt("my_array.csv", delimiter=",")

NumPy Indexing & Selection Hour 1–2

Indexing a 2D array. Just like a list, but now with two coordinates — think of it as "row, column," exactly like reading a spreadsheet cell reference:

grid = np.array([[10, 20, 30],
                  [40, 50, 60],
                  [70, 80, 90]])

print(grid[0, 0])     # 10 — row 0, column 0
print(grid[1, 2])     # 60 — row 1, column 2
print(grid[0])         # [10 20 30] — the entire first row
print(grid[:, 1])      # [20 50 80] — the entire second column (: means "all rows")
print(grid[0:2, 0:2])  # top-left 2x2 sub-grid — slicing works in both dimensions

Logical (boolean) selection — instead of picking values by position, you pick them by a condition, which is one of the most powerful and commonly used NumPy features in real data work:

scores = np.array([55, 90, 40, 78, 62, 95])

# this comparison creates an array of True/False values, one per element
passing_mask = scores >= 60
print(passing_mask)   # [False  True False  True  True  True]

# using that mask to pull out only the matching values
passing_scores = scores[passing_mask]
print(passing_scores)   # [90 78 62 95]

# the same thing, in one line — the idiomatic style
print(scores[scores >= 60])

Broadcasting Hour 1–2

The problem it solves. What happens if you want to add a single number to every element of an array at once, or add a small array to a bigger one? Doing this with a manual loop works, but is slow and verbose. Broadcasting is NumPy's rule for automatically "stretching" a smaller array to match a bigger one's shape, so the operation just works, element by element, without you writing a loop.

arr = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4])

# broadcasting a single number across every element
print(arr + 10)      # [11 12 13 14] — 10 is "stretched" to match arr's shape

# broadcasting a smaller array across a bigger one
grid = np.array([[1, 2, 3],
                  [4, 5, 6]])
row = np.array([10, 20, 30])
print(grid + row)     # row is added to EVERY row of grid automatically
# [[11 22 33]
#  [14 25 36]]
Why this matters for ML Broadcasting is exactly how you'd normalize an entire dataset in one line — subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation of every column at once, without a single explicit loop. It's central to writing fast, "vectorized" data-science code.

Type Casting Hour 3–4

Every NumPy array has one single data type for all its elements (unlike a Python list, which can mix types freely). Type casting converts an array from one type to another — commonly needed when loading messy real-world data, or when a specific calculation requires floats instead of integers.

int_arr = np.array([1, 2, 3])
float_arr = int_arr.astype(np.float64)
print(float_arr)      # [1. 2. 3.]
print(float_arr.dtype) # float64

Arithmetic Operations & Universal Array Functions Hour 3–4

Element-wise arithmetic — every basic operator works directly on whole arrays at once, applying to each matching pair of elements (this is broadcasting's simplest form, seen already above):

a = np.array([10, 20, 30])
b = np.array([1, 2, 3])

print(a + b)   # [11 22 33]
print(a - b)   # [9 18 27]
print(a * b)   # [10 40 90]
print(a / b)   # [10. 10. 10.]
print(a ** 2)  # [100 400 900]

Universal functions ("ufuncs") — NumPy's own fast, built-in math functions, applied to an entire array at once:

arr = np.array([1, 4, 9, 16])

print(np.sqrt(arr))    # [1. 2. 3. 4.]
print(np.exp(arr))      # e raised to the power of each element
print(np.log(arr))      # natural log of each element
print(np.sin(arr))      # sine of each element
print(np.max(arr))      # 16
Best practice Always prefer NumPy's vectorized operations (a + b, np.sqrt(arr)) over writing a manual Python for loop to do the same thing element by element — the vectorized version is both far shorter to write and dramatically faster to run.

Introduction to Pandas

Hour 1–4 · Series & DataFrame · Selection & indexing · DataFrame operations · Missing data handling

Introduction to Pandas — From Grids of Numbers to Real Tables Hour 1

The gap NumPy leaves open. NumPy arrays are fantastic for pure numbers, but real-world data isn't just numbers — it's a spreadsheet with named columns ("Name", "Age", "City"), mixed types (text and numbers together), and often missing values. Pandas (built on top of NumPy) adds exactly this: labeled rows and columns, and a huge toolbox of convenience methods for cleaning and exploring data — it is, without exaggeration, the single most-used library in everyday data science work.

import pandas as pd   # the universal convention: import pandas as pd

Series and DataFrame, and Loading Data Hour 2

A Series is a single labeled column of data — think of it as one column from a spreadsheet, with an index (row label) attached to every value.

ages = pd.Series([25, 30, 35], index=["Ayesha", "Bilal", "Sara"])
print(ages)
# Ayesha    25
# Bilal     30
# Sara      35
print(ages["Bilal"])   # 30 — look up by label, not just position

A DataFrame is the full spreadsheet — a 2D table made of multiple Series (columns) that all share the same row index. This is the object you'll work with constantly for the rest of the course.

data = {
    "name": ["Ayesha", "Bilal", "Sara"],
    "age": [25, 30, 35],
    "city": ["Lahore", "Karachi", "Islamabad"]
}
df = pd.DataFrame(data)
print(df)

Loading real data — in practice, you'll almost never type a DataFrame by hand; you'll load it from a file:

df = pd.read_csv("students.csv")
df_excel = pd.read_excel("students.xlsx")

Selection and Indexing Hour 2

# selecting a single column — returns a Series
print(df["name"])

# selecting multiple columns — returns a DataFrame
print(df[["name", "age"]])

# selecting rows by label with .loc, or by position with .iloc
print(df.loc[0])          # the row labeled 0
print(df.iloc[0])          # the row at position 0 — same result here, different logic

# conditional selection — the Pandas version of NumPy's boolean masking
adults = df[df["age"] >= 30]
print(adults)

# selecting a subset of rows AND columns at once
print(df.loc[df["age"] >= 30, ["name", "city"]])

# setting a different column as the index
df_indexed = df.set_index("name")
print(df_indexed.loc["Ayesha"])
.loc vs .iloc, remembered simply .loc = "location by LABEL" (the name of the row/column). .iloc = "location by INTEGER position" (0, 1, 2...), regardless of what the labels actually say. This distinction becomes important once you set a custom index, like a student's name.

Operations on DataFrames Hour 3

print(df.head())                 # first 5 rows — a quick first look at any dataset
print(df["city"].unique())        # every distinct value in a column
print(df["city"].value_counts())  # how many times each value appears

# applying your own custom function to every value in a column
df["age_in_5_years"] = df["age"].apply(lambda x: x + 5)

print(df.columns)      # the column names
print(df.index)         # the row labels

df_sorted = df.sort_values("age")             # sort rows by a column
df.replace("Lahore", "LHR", inplace=True)      # find-and-replace a value

df_dropped = df.drop(columns=["city"])          # drop a column
df_dropped_row = df.drop(index=0)                # drop a row
Common mistake Most Pandas operations return a new DataFrame by default rather than modifying the original — df.sort_values("age") doesn't change df itself unless you either reassign it (df = df.sort_values("age")) or pass inplace=True. Forgetting this is one of the most common beginner bugs in Pandas.

Missing Data & Its Handling Hour 4

Why this is unavoidable. Real-world data is messy — a survey respondent skips a question, a sensor briefly fails, a form field is left blank. Pandas represents a missing value as NaN ("Not a Number"), and handling it properly is one of the most important, unglamorous parts of any real data project.

import numpy as np
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "name": ["Ayesha", "Bilal", "Sara"],
    "score": [85, np.nan, 90]
})

print(df.isnull())          # True/False table showing exactly where data is missing
print(df.isnull().sum())     # count of missing values per column

# option 1: drop rows with any missing value
df_dropped = df.dropna()

# option 2: fill missing values with something sensible
df_filled_zero = df.fillna(0)
df_filled_mean = df.fillna(df["score"].mean())   # fill with the column's mean — Week 2 knowledge, applied!
How to choose between dropping and filling Drop rows only if missing data is rare and random. If a column has a lot of missing values, dropping every affected row can throw away too much good data — filling with the mean, median, or a sensible default is usually the better choice, and exactly why last week's central tendency measures matter in practice, not just in theory.

Compute the correlation between the two columns, use NumPy to calculate the mean, standard deviation, and Z-scores of the exam scores, and flag any student whose Z-score is above 2 or below −2 as a notable outlier.

Industry Project

Spam Probability Estimator. Using a small labeled dataset of emails marked spam/not-spam, and a short list of "trigger words," compute the conditional probability that an email is spam given it contains a specific trigger word, using counts and Bayes' theorem by hand (no external ML library) — the same core logic behind real-world Naive Bayes spam filters.
